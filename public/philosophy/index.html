Philosophy

In brief. I want to live and work in a way that is whole. I believe modern education fragments us from ourselves, one another and the living world, so I try to rewild it, through relational, dialogic, embodied pedagogies. Because work shapes so much of life, I want my work to hold this too. I look for small, deep places to act, where the effect can ripple outward.

Starting from the kitchen

I don’t have time to segregate waste at home. My partner loves cooking, but she rarely has the time or energy for the things that make her feel alive. We are both educators and both do social work, giving our days to something larger than ourselves. Even so, the small daily choices slip. Every day we try to find meaning in an absurd world, and to find the energy and time to make good choices, or at least informed ones, about our relationships with place, money, water, people and the more-than-human world around us.

Some questions I cannot put down: What is the right way to live? Money or meaning? Scale or deeper impact? Comfort or eco-friendliness? City or village? Is there a middle way, and can anyone learn to live in balance?

The fragmentation

The longer I live with these questions, the more they look like one question. We are living through an interconnected meta-crisis, personal, social and ecological, and at its deepest level I believe it is a crisis of consciousness. It comes from fragmentation: our relationships have changed. How can we understand ourselves, experience our interconnectedness with the world, and act with care for everyone’s wellbeing, so that we live in harmony with existence?

Modern education is one of the biggest influences on all of these relationships. Its aim is to prepare humans for the economy and the workforce, and nature, community, and social and ecological wellbeing are not part of its design. The result is a series of disconnections:

  • from oneself
  • from community and society
  • from Earth, biosphere and land
  • between subjects and disciplines
  • between “learning” and real-world engagement

These disconnections alienate us from the world. Think about a child who doesn’t know where food comes from, where our waste goes, or who all our neighbors are, human and more-than-human. Think about a child who is exposed only to what is important for marks, exams and career. How can someone like this act in harmony?

My deeper question is this: is there a way of living, personal and social, that is not conflict-ridden but essentially peaceful and whole? And what role can education and creative practice play in it?

Work as an anchor

Work takes up, on average, ten hours of our waking day. It earns the money we live on, gives us meaning, and gives us the physical energy and freedom for everything else.

If work is that large, it cannot sit outside our values. It has to be the place where they are practiced. So I ask whether my work can anchor personal, social and ecological wellbeing together.

I find the clearest language for this in alivelihood, a term from Manish Jain and Kalashree Sengupta of Swaraj University and the Ecoversities Alliance.

An alivelihood is work in which our soul comes alive, and which also benefits the community and ecosystem we live in. It is the opposite of a “deadlihood,” work that is soul-sucking and extractive. Jain and Sengupta describe four circles of an alivelihood, and they note that it is a way to merge dharma and dhandhaa, one’s calling and one’s trade. (Read the original article ↗)

I want to live as an educator and a creative practitioner, and for me the two cannot be separated. What kind of education I facilitate, and in what form, matters as much as how I live. The medium I choose and what I write or perform matter in the same way.

Rewilding education

My journey as an educator and creative practitioner began as pure exploration and experimentation. Meeting people, working in different places, reading and reflecting shaped my philosophy.

Along the way, someone introduced me to integral education, and others to Martin Buber, J. Krishnamurti, Gandhi and Waldorf. My work is an attempt to be an antidote to an education built for the economy, by rewilding the disconnections that the machinery of schooling has created. It comes from a humanist, existential, experiential lineage.

Education is the practice of meeting, relating authentically with ourselves, one another, our places, and the more-than-human world, through looking, listening, questioning, reflecting, and creating together.

Learning with head, heart, hands and soul, we deepen our understanding of ourselves and our interconnections. We cultivate the sensitivity, critical discernment and courage to act freely, responsibly and with care in a shared world. Three principles follow.

Learning should be dialogic, reciprocal and inquiry-based

The teacher is not the sole authority or a transmitter of knowledge. Learning happens when teacher and students go through the process of questioning, sharing, listening and exploring together. Learning does not happen through teaching; it happens through meeting, the meeting of I and Thou (Martin Buber).

Learning should be relational, contextual and interdisciplinary

Learning involves building deeper relationships with oneself, with others and the wider society, with place, and with the more-than-human world.

Learning should be experiential, embodied and action-based

Learning should happen with the whole being of a person, engaging head, heart, hand and soul. Experiments, projects, yatras, activities, inquiries, films and music can create opportunities for learning to be contextual, experienced, felt and lived.

What do I hope children and young adults carry away? A developed sense of the senses, a friendship with their own mind, good judgment and critical thinking, and the essence of community living. Flexibility, freedom and creativity. The learnability, sensitivity and empathy to keep growing. The ability to make a livelihood from their own gifts. And an expansion of joyfulness.

How do I facilitate this? The best answer I know comes from one of my teachers, Mahendra Chotalia:

Do you wish to be a genuine teacher? Here is your responsibility: Enter into the being of the person in your student. Accept him/her unconditionally. Create a space around by enabling them to see and remove the pressure of assumptions and beliefs. Encourage them to risk flights in the unknown, seamless life. Reflect together on the learning-unlearning from that flight. Enjoy the friendship.

Mahendra Chotalia

My programs, workshops and interventions are only an anchor. My intention is to create a space for dialogue and co-creation, an incubation space for everyone, where we learn, experiment, challenge ourselves, break barriers and grow. With young children (ages 3 to 11) I use art, theater, music, movement, nature exploration, short inquiry-based projects and children’s literature. With young adults (ages 12 and above) I use interdisciplinary projects (entrepreneurship, inquiry-based and civic action projects), nature exploration, theater, third spaces (places that are neither home nor school nor work), yatra and more.

Where I want to focus

I want to work more in guided learning spaces. I began as an educator in schools and in formal and informal learning spaces. That gradually shifted to teacher capacity building, curriculum design, and work with pre-service teachers and researchers. My quest now is to find places where I can stay in direct touch with children, so I can keep practicing; where I can work with self-motivated educators and help them develop their own flavor of rewilding; and where I can find some financial stability.

I believe in the “butterfly effect.” Read How I engineer it ↗.

Practice ground / engagement with children: I prefer to work with the same group of children regularly. A deep intervention with a learning center or school, or setting up our own school, is the ideal situation for me.

Engagement with educators: I am tired of doing teacher “training” workshops where teachers come just for formality or compulsion. I want to work with seekers, self-directed learners and inquirers who really want to question the aim of education and our way of living.

I am also interested in long-term, deeper engagement with pre-service teachers (B.Ed, PTC, D.El.Ed, MSc in child development and similar courses).

I am also interested in engagement with NGOs and learning spaces that want real transformation and not just quick fixes.

As an artist, most of my creative practice is part of my educational practice. Publishing a children’s magazine is one example. I write stories and poems and work with illustrators, and after publication I work with teachers and directly with children through capacity-building workshops and storytelling.

To summarize: I want to practice a harmonious way of living, and I try to do so by paying attention to my day-to-day relationships and actions. Because work is such a big part of life, I want my work to carry this way of being too. So I choose paths where a small, deep act can have an impact I could not plan for.
